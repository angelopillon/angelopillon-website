Mixing on headphones: how to get a mix that also works on speakers

Read inItalianoEnglishDeutsch

On headphones each ear hears only its own channel, the low end deceives you and after an hour everything sounds right. You can still mix well, with a few rules.

By Angelo Pillon 5 November 2026 4 min read
On this page

On this page

  1. What changes compared with speakers
  2. Which headphones to use
  3. Correction, simulation and crossfeed
  4. The method I recommend

In short
  • Headphones lack the room and the sound crossing from one ear to the other: stereo seems wider, reverbs more obvious, and the lows are heard but not felt in the body.
  • Open-back headphones, a low and constant volume, matched-loudness references and checks on other systems solve most of the problems.
  • Response correction and crossfeed help, but they do not replace familiarity with a pair you know well.

Many of the mixes I receive are born on headphones: in a bedroom, while travelling, at night when the speakers cannot be turned on. It is not a limitation to hide. Some excellent producers work almost only this way. But headphones have their own way of describing the mix, and if you do not know it, they lead you astray in the same places every time.

What changes compared with speakers

Stereo seems wider. With speakers, each ear hears both speakers, with a small delay and the filtering of the head. On headphones, the left ear hears only the left channel. An element panned halfway seems very far to the side, stereo wideners seem more effective than they are, and the centre of the mix feels inside your head instead of in front of you.

Reverbs are more audible. Details that a room masks are in the foreground on headphones. The typical result is a mix with reverbs and delays set too low, which sounds dry on speakers.

The low end deceives. A good pair of headphones reproduces the subs better than many untreated rooms, but you do not feel them in your body. And it is easy to get the amount of bass wrong, in one direction or the other, depending on the headphones and how they sit on your head.

The vocal tends to stay too low. On headphones you understand every word even at low volume. On speakers, with the rest of the mix filling the room, the same vocal gets lost.

Your ears tire sooner. The pressure is close to the eardrum and the volume tends to creep up as the hours pass. After a while everything sounds right, whether it is or not.

Which headphones to use

For mixing, open-back headphones are the better choice: they have a more natural response and fewer resonances than closed ones. In studios you often see models such as the Sennheiser HD 600 and HD 650 or the Beyerdynamic DT 880; for recording you need closed headphones instead, such as the Beyerdynamic DT 770 Pro or the Sony MDR-7506, which do not leak sound into the microphone.

That said, the best headphones are the ones you know. A pair you have listened to for months with your reference records is worth more than a more expensive pair used for a week.

Correction, simulation and crossfeed

There is software that measures the average response of a headphone model and corrects it to make it flatter, and there are plugins that simulate listening to speakers in a room. Crossfeed, the simpler option, sends a little of each channel to the other ear, with a small delay and a filter, and reduces the exaggerated stereo.

These tools are useful, especially at the start. Use them as an aid, not as the truth: now and then, listen without correction too, and learn how your mix sounds on the bare headphones.

On headphones the problem is not hearing too little. It is hearing the wrong things too well and the things that matter on speakers too little.

The method I recommend

  1. Low, constant volume. Pick a comfortable listening level and keep it for the whole session. Take a break of a few minutes every hour.
  2. References at matched loudness. Listen to two or three tracks in your genre at the same perceived level as your mix. With the A/B reference player you switch between them without gaps and with the level already matched.
  3. Crossfeed or correction, if they help you.
  4. Check the lows with your eyes as well as your ears. Compare the spectrum and the short-term loudness of your drop with the references, and check that the subs are mono with the stereo phase analyzer.
  5. Mono check. On headphones it matters even more, because exaggerated stereo hides phase problems. The tutorial on mono compatibility explains how to do it.
  6. Test on other systems. Phone, earbuds, car, a Bluetooth speaker. Note what changes, go back to the headphones, fix it.
  7. Listen again the next day. Fresh ears immediately spot what had disappeared after five hours.

The typical mistakes of headphone mixes

  • Reverbs and delays set too low.
  • Exaggerated stereo wideners that fall apart on speakers and in mono.
  • Bass or sub out of proportion, almost always in the same direction for the same headphones.
  • A vocal slightly low against the beat.
  • Highs pushed too far, because at low volume they seem to be missing.

If you know your headphones’ tendency, you can correct for it in advance. That is the advantage of always using the same pair.
