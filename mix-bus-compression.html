Mix bus compression, step by step

Read inItalianoEnglishDeutsch

One compressor on the mix bus doing 1 to 2 dB is what makes separate tracks sound like one song. The trick is to put it on early and let it work gently.

By Angelo Pillon 9 November 2026 3 min read

Time20 minutesLevelIntermediateYou needA bus compressor and a loudness meter

Mix bus compression is not about loudness: that is mastering’s job. It is about making the tracks move together, as if they were playing in the same room. In my studio the bus goes through an SSL-style VCA compressor; in the DAW any well-designed bus compressor works.

The procedure

  1. Put it on early, not at the end

    Insert the compressor once the rough balance is in place. If you add it to a finished mix, it shifts every balance you built and you will redo half the work.

  2. Low ratio

    Start at 2:1. At 4:1 the compression is more audible and useful in aggressive music, but 2:1 is almost always enough to glue.

  3. Slow attack to let transients through

    Between 10 and 30 ms. With an attack that is too fast, kick and snare lose their snap and the mix flattens.

  4. Timed or auto release

    Between 100 and 300 ms, or auto. The needle should return to zero between hits, in time with the song: if it stays down all the time, the release is too long.

  5. Filter the sidechain

    If the compressor allows it, high-pass the sidechain at 80 to 100 Hz. The low end then stops driving the whole compression and the mix does not pump on every kick.

  6. Threshold for 1 to 2 dB of gain reduction

    Lower the threshold until the needle moves 1 to 2 dB on the loudest parts. Dense electronic music can take 3 dB, rarely more.

  7. Compare at matched loudness

    Compensate the output gain and bypass. Without the compressor the mix should feel a little looser, not louder. If it sounds better in bypass, reduce the gain reduction.

Starting values

Parameter Value
Ratio 2:1, up to 4:1 for aggressive music
Attack 10 to 30 ms
Release 100 to 300 ms or auto
Sidechain filter high-pass at 80 to 100 Hz
Gain reduction 1 to 2 dB, 3 at most
A bus compressor should move with the song, not against it. If it sits at −4 dB all the time, it is just turning the mix down.

Mixing for mastering

If the track is going to a mastering engineer, keep the bus compressor only if it is part of the sound you want. What should not stay is a limiter pushing the mix to 0 dBFS: it takes away the room mastering needs. You will find the full list in premaster mistakes.
