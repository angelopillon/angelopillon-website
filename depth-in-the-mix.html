Depth in the mix: how to give a track three dimensions

Read inItalianoEnglishDeutsch

Left and right are easy. Front and back are what make a mix feel real. Here are the tools I use to build them.

By Angelo Pillon 5 November 2026 4 min read
On this page

On this page

  1. The five cues of distance
  2. Pre-delay: close, but inside a space
  3. The method I use

In short
  • The ear judges distance from five cues: level, amount of high frequencies, sharpness of transients, the ratio of direct sound to reverb, and early reflections.
  • Decide first who stands in front: two or three elements, not all of them. Depth comes from contrast.
  • A few shared reverbs, filtered and with tempo-synced pre-delay, give more depth than a different reverb on every track.

A flat mix is not necessarily a wrong mix: everything is audible, nothing clashes. But it sounds like a photo without perspective. Depth is what makes a track feel like a place, with a vocal speaking to you up close and a pad breathing behind it.

Stereo works horizontally, from left to right, and it is the tool everyone reaches for first. Depth works front to back, and it is built with different tools.

The five cues of distance

Level. A louder sound seems closer. It is the most obvious cue and the least sufficient: turning an element down pushes it back, but on its own it also makes it smaller.

High frequencies. In air, high frequencies fade with distance more than low ones. A darker sound seems further away. A gentle low-pass filter or a shelf taking a few dB off above 8–10 kHz pushes an element back naturally.

Transients. Up close the attack of a sound is sharp; from a distance it softens. A compressor with a fast attack or a transient designer that reduces the attack pushes an element back; a sharper attack brings it forward.

The direct-to-reverb ratio. More direct sound and less reverb means closeness. More reverb, with the same direct sound, means distance. This is the heart of depth.

Early reflections. The reflections that arrive in the first tenths of a second tell the ear how big the room is and where the source is. The reverb tail tells the atmosphere, not the position.

Pre-delay: close, but inside a space

Pre-delay is the time between the direct sound and the start of the reverb. A long pre-delay, between 20 and 60 ms, separates the vocal from its space: you hear it close, with the room behind. A short or zero pre-delay immerses it in the room and pushes it away.

Setting it in time with the track helps the reverb move with the groove instead of muddying it. The delay and reverb calculator gives you pre-delay and decay in milliseconds for the track’s BPM, for rooms of different sizes.

Depth comes from contrast. If everything is in front, nothing is in front.

The method I use

  1. I decide the planes. Before touching a reverb, I split the elements into three groups: front (vocal, kick, snare, the main element), middle (bass, guitars, rhythmic synths), back (pads, backing vocals, textures, effects). Two or three elements belong in front, not ten.
  2. I set up a few shared reverbs. Usually a short room to glue the mix, a plate for vocal and snare, a long reverb for the background elements. Sending many tracks to the same reverbs makes them sound as if they play in the same place.
  3. I filter the returns. A high-pass between 200 and 300 Hz and a low-pass between 6 and 10 kHz on the returns: the reverb stays, the mud and the harshness do not.
  4. I push back with highs and transients, not just with level. To push a pad back I take off some highs and some attack before turning it down.
  5. I bring forward with contrast. The front vocal stays drier and brighter than the elements behind it, with a pre-delay that separates it from its space.
  6. I automate the depth. A drier, more intimate verse, a wider chorus, a break where everything moves away: depth that changes over time makes the track breathe.

Depth and stereo together

In reality, distant elements occupy a narrower angle. A very distant pad can be narrower than you think, while a reverb can be wide. The lead vocal, centred and dry, stands out precisely because there is width around it.

Watch out for what happens in mono, though: many stereo reverbs are low in correlation and can hollow out in mono. The tutorial on phase and mono compatibility explains how to check it.

What changes from genre to genre

  • Techno and house: depth lives in the space around the kick. Kick and bass dry and in front, percussion and synths moving away, long reverbs opening up in the breaks.
  • Pop: the vocal in front of everything, with a plate and generous pre-delay, pads and backing vocals behind, drums in a short room.
  • R&B and soul: an intimate vocal, very close, often with short reverbs and soft delays, in a mix that leaves a lot of space between the elements.
  • Rap and trap: the vocal in front and dry, with depth built more from tempo-synced delays than from reverbs. I cover it in the article on rap and trap vocals.
