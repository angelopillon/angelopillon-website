Subtractive EQ: why cutting before boosting makes a mix breathe

Read inItalianoEnglishDeutsch

Almost every crowded mix has the same problem: too many tracks living in the same frequencies. Cutting frees space without adding level, and the mix opens up on its own.

By Angelo Pillon 9 November 2026 3 min read
On this page

On this page

  1. The high-pass, with judgement
  2. The 200 to 500 Hz zone
  3. Resonances: narrow bell, decisive cut
  4. When to boost
  5. Common mistakes
In short
  • Cutting moves space around without adding energy: the mix stays clean and the limiter works less.
  • Set the high-pass where the track stops being useful, not at one fixed frequency on everything.
  • Find resonances with a narrow bell and cut them by 2 to 6 dB, by ear, not by staring at the analyzer.

When a vocal gets lost, the first instinct is to turn it up or add presence at 3 kHz. Often the vocal is not the problem: guitars, pads and piano are sitting in the same zone. Subtractive EQ makes room for the vocal instead of making it shout over everyone else.

The high-pass, with judgement

The high-pass filter is the most useful cut and the most misused. Putting it at 100 Hz on every track out of habit thins the mix. My rule: raise the filter until the track, heard in the mix and not in solo, starts losing body, then back off a little.

  • Vocals: usually between 70 and 150 Hz.
  • Guitars and piano in a full mix: between 100 and 200 Hz, often higher than you think.
  • Pads and synths: as high as needed for the low end to belong to bass and kick.
  • Overheads and percussion: depends on how much kick they pick up.

Use gentle slopes, 12 or 18 dB per octave. A 48 dB per octave filter rings at the cutoff and can create a bump that was not there before.

The 200 to 500 Hz zone

This is where mud builds up. Every track has something there, and together they make a mix that sounds boxy and closed. You do not need to empty it, just choose who owns it. I usually keep the body on bass, vocal and one main harmonic instrument, and take 2 to 4 dB out of the others with a wide bell.

Every dB you take from one track is space you give to another. The mix does not get louder, but everything gets clearer.

Resonances: narrow bell, decisive cut

Some tracks have a whistle or a boom that jumps out on certain notes: a ringing room, a kick that sounds like a cardboard box. To find them I boost a narrow bell by 8 to 10 dB, sweep slowly until the flaw explodes, then pull it below zero: 2 to 6 dB of cut, narrow Q. One or two resonances per track, not ten. If you find ten, the problem is the recording.

When to boost

Additive EQ is not forbidden. It works when a track is recorded well but needs to sit in a precise place: air on a vocal, attack on a kick. It works better after cutting: with less masking, 1 or 2 dB do what 6 dB did before.

Common mistakes

  • EQing in solo. The track has to sound right in the mix, not on its own.
  • Watching the analyzer instead of listening.
  • Comparing at different levels: a cut seems to make things worse just because it is quieter. Match the gain and compare at equal loudness.

If the whole mix feels unbalanced, check your gain staging before reaching for EQ: a lot of mud comes from tracks hitting plugins too hot.
