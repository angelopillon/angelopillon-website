Producers who have put some money aside often write to me asking what to spend it on. A new interface? Bigger monitors? A serious microphone? My answer is rarely the one they expect, because the bottleneck of a home studio is almost never the most expensive object in the room.

<p class="disclaimer">Personal opinion, based on my work: I have no sponsorship or affiliation with manufacturers or shops. I use dollars because gear prices are usually compared in that currency; in euros the order of magnitude is similar.</p>

## The order in which to spend

1. **The room.** Bass traps in the corners, panels at the first reflection points, ideally something above the listening position. Monitors and head at the corners of an equilateral triangle, speakers away from the corners. It is the purchase with the best ratio of cost to result, and the one most often postponed.
2. **Monitoring.** Monitors suited to the size of the room: in a small room a big woofer creates more problems than lows. And a pair of headphones you know well, to check what the room hides.
3. **A reliable interface.** Stable drivers, low latency, clean converters. The mid range today is more than enough: what matters more is that it does not freeze in the middle of a session.
4. **Computer and software.** Enough power that you never have to stop, the DAW you know, a few good plugins: an EQ, a compressor, a limiter, a reverb. Learned in depth, they are worth more than a hundred folders of presets.
5. **Microphone and preamp, if you record vocals.** A good vocal mic, in the right position and in a quiet, treated room, matters more than an expensive preamp.

## Up to 8–9 thousand dollars: every choice shows

In this range every improvement removes a real limitation. Treating the room lets you hear the lows for what they are. Suitable monitors let you understand where the vocal really sits. A stable interface lets you work without interruptions. It does not improve the sound of the gear: it improves your judgement, and judgement is what ends up on the record.

## Between 9 and 40 thousand dollars: the zone of flat returns

Past that threshold, the limits move elsewhere: to the room, which should be built rather than treated, to experience, to the time you spend listening. The difference between a good converter and one from a much higher tier exists, and in the studio you can hear it if you listen carefully. On a track that has been mixed, mastered and played on streaming, the listener does not perceive it.

In this range, in my view, money goes further when it is spent on training, on professional feedback on your work, or on the mixing and mastering of the tracks that matter.

<blockquote class="pull">Up to a certain figure, buying gear improves your judgement. After that, it mostly improves how the studio looks in photos.</blockquote>

## Beyond 40 thousand dollars: changing category

Here we are no longer talking about “improving” a home studio, but about turning it into a different kind of studio. It makes sense when the work demands it:

- an **analog console**, if you mix a lot of tracks in analog or record live bands;
- multi-channel **SSL or Neve preamps**, if you record drums and groups with many microphones;
- **outboard** for a hybrid mixing or mastering chain;
- **high-end converters**, to send the signal out to analog and back without losses;
- **a room designed from scratch**, with calculated isolation and acoustics.

It is a different job, with different clients and different costs. If your work is producing and mixing your own tracks, you almost certainly do not need it.

## What not to buy, at least for now

- Plugin bundles you will not have time to learn.
- Monitors too big for the room.
- An expensive microphone before treating the room.
- A new interface, if the problem is monitoring.
- Pre-packaged mastering chains.
