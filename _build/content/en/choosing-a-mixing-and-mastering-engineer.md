Today you can have a track mixed or mastered by an engineer on the other side of the world as easily as by a studio down the road. That is great news, but it makes the choice harder: there are thousands of offers, the studio photos all look alike and prices range from a few dollars to serious figures. These are the criteria I would use if I had to choose someone for one of my own tracks.

## Listen to work in your genre

An engineer can be excellent in rock and unconvincing in trap, or the other way round. Every genre has its own rules: the lows of techno, the vocal of pop, the density of rap. Look for work in your genre and, if possible, listen to the before and after: it is the only way to understand how much the engineer did and how much was already in the mix.

Listen at low volume and then at normal volume, on headphones and on speakers. A good master sounds right in both cases.

## Read reviews the right way

Reviews on verified platforms, such as Fiverr or SoundBetter, are more reliable than hand-picked quotes on a personal website. Do not stop at the average: look at how many clients came back several times, read the negative reviews and how the engineer replied. A professional who handles a problem well is worth more than one who has never shown one.

## Ask how they work

You do not need to become technical, but a few questions say a lot:

- **How do they decide loudness?** Be wary of anyone who answers with one fixed number for everyone. The right loudness depends on the genre and the destination; I cover it in [LUFS and streaming in 2026](/lufs-streaming-targets-2026).
- **Do they check the files after streaming encoding?** It is a detail that separates those who think about it from those who do not.
- **Do they work with references?** Asking for your reference tracks is the first sign that the engineer wants to understand what you have in mind.
- **Is the mastering done by a person or an algorithm?** Automatic mastering is useful for a demo; a real release needs someone who listens to your track and makes choices.

## Revisions: how many and how

Ask beforehand how many revisions are included, what counts as a revision and what happens if the result does not convince you. Policies vary a lot: some include one or two, others keep going until the track is right. There is no single best rule: there is a clear rule, written down before you start.

<blockquote class="pull">The right price is not the lowest. It is the one you know before you start, with everything you need included.</blockquote>

## Delivery: formats, versions, turnaround

A master should be delivered at least as a WAV at the resolution suited to distribution. If the track is also going to DJs or clubs, ask whether a dedicated version is included. Find out which versions are included and which cost extra, and how long it takes: mastering usually takes a few days, a mix a few more.

## Communication

Write to the engineer before sending the track and see how they reply. How long they take, whether they ask questions about your project, whether they explain things in a way you can understand. You will be working with this person for days, sometimes for years: communication matters as much as gear.

## Prices and rights

Be wary of prices that do not say what they include, and of offers so low they cannot cover the time needed for careful listening. Remember too that the music stays yours: the engineer is paid for a service and acquires no rights to the track. If someone asks for percentages or rights for a mix or a master, it is time to ask questions.

<div class="box"><span class="box__title">Questions to ask before you start</span>
<ul>
<li>Have you worked on tracks in my genre? Can I hear some?</li>
<li>How much does it cost, and what is included?</li>
<li>How many revisions are there, and what counts as a revision?</li>
<li>How long does delivery take, and in which formats?</li>
<li>How do you decide loudness, and do you check the files for streaming?</li>
<li>How do you prefer to receive the tracks?</li>
</ul></div>
