Ozone is a serious tool, not a toy. The question is not whether it is good, but what job you are asking it to do.

<figure><img src="/assets/img/photo/hands-knobs.webp" alt="Angelo Pillon’s hands on the mastering outboard controls" width="640" height="383" loading="lazy" decoding="async"><figcaption>Mastering decisions are made by ear, one knob at a time. Studio photo.</figcaption></figure>

## What Ozone 12 does well

**Stem EQ.** EQ vocals, drums, bass and instruments inside a stereo file. Useful when you do not have the tracks.

**Unlimiter.** It tries to bring back the transients of a file that has already been squashed.

**IRC 5.** The Maximizer’s new algorithm for more level with less pumping.

**Master Assistant.** You now decide how it intervenes, instead of accepting a ready-made chain.

## The comparison

| | Ozone 12 | Mastering engineer |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | one-off, from $55 | per track |
| Turnaround | minutes | from 48 hours |
| Verdict on the mix | no | yes, before starting |
| Listening | your monitors | a reference room and chain |
| Album consistency | up to you | part of the job |
| Revisions | you do them | you ask for them |

## When AI is enough and when it is not

For a demo, a track you want online tonight or for learning, Ozone is fine. For a label release, an EP that has to hang together or a song you have spent months on, you need an ear that has not been living with the track. Often the problem is not in the master but in the mix, and no assistant will tell you that.

<blockquote class="pull">AI tells you where the frequencies are. An engineer tells you whether the song works.</blockquote>

If you mastered it yourself, at least check the true peak: I stay between -0.3 and -0.2 dBTP with a true peak limiter, as I explain in the article on [true peak](/true-peak-minus-1-dbtp).
