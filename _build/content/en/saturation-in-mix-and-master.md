<div class="tldr"><span class="box__title">In short</span>
<ul>
<li>Saturation adds harmonics: it makes a sound more present, even on small systems.</li>
<li>Tape and transformers round things off, tubes and clippers add bite.</li>
<li>Always judge it at matched loudness: louder always seems better.</li>
</ul></div>

A clean bass at 50 Hz disappears on a phone. The same bass with a little saturation generates harmonics at 100, 150 and 200 Hz, and the ear rebuilds the fundamental even where the speaker cannot reach. That is the practical reason saturation is in almost every professional mix.

## The main types

| Type | Character | Where I use it |
|---|---|---|
| Tape | Rounds transients, tightens the low end | Drum bus, mix bus, master |
| Transformer | Subtle thickness in the low mids | Bass, vocal, buses |
| Tube | Even harmonics, warmth and presence | Vocal, synths, pads |
| Clipper | Shaves peaks, adds bite | Kick, drums, before the limiter |

## In the mix: a little, in many places

I prefer a little saturation on several tracks to a lot on one. A touch on the bass so it translates on small speakers, some on the vocal to keep it forward, tape on the drum bus to glue it. Each stage adds a little, and the mix as a whole gains density.

On bass-heavy tracks like bass and 808, check phase after saturating: the new harmonics can change how kick and bass sum together.

<blockquote class="pull">If you hear the distortion as an effect, you have already gone past the sweet spot. Good saturation is what you miss when you switch it off.</blockquote>

## In the master: even less

In mastering, saturation is about cohesion and about how the track holds up as it gets louder. In my chain that is analog tape, which tightens the lows and softens peaks before the limiter; I explain why in [analog mastering](/analog-mastering). A light clipper before the limiter takes off the sharpest transients so the limiter can work less.

## How to set it

1. Insert the saturator and raise the drive until you clearly hear the change.
2. Back it off by half.
3. Match the output level and compare in bypass at equal loudness.
4. Listen quietly: if the sound stays present without getting harsh, you are in the right place.

## Signs you are overdoing it

- The highs turn gritty and tire your ears within minutes.
- The kick loses its attack and becomes a thud.
- Cymbals and sibilants start to fizz.
- The mix sounds louder but less deep.
