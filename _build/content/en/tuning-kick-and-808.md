<div class="howto-meta"><span><b>Time</b>25 minutes</span><span><b>Level</b>Intermediate</span><span><b>You need</b>A sampler, a spectrum analyzer and the <a href="/tools/kick-808-tuning">tuning tool</a></span></div>

In rumble techno, trap, drill and much of hip hop, the kick and the 808 are not just rhythm: they have a precise pitch, and that pitch talks to the rest of the track. If the kick plays an E while the track is in F minor, the low end clashes. Nobody will say “the kick is out of tune”, but everyone will hear a less solid foundation.

Very short kicks, almost all attack, have little recognisable pitch and usually do not need tuning. This tutorial is for kicks with body and tail, and for any 808.

## The most used low notes

| Note | Frequency | Note | Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | 32.7 Hz | G1 | 49.0 Hz |
| D1 | 36.7 Hz | A1 | 55.0 Hz |
| E1 | 41.2 Hz | B1 | 61.7 Hz |
| F1 | 43.7 Hz | C2 | 65.4 Hz |
| F♯1 | 46.2 Hz | D2 | 73.4 Hz |

With the reference A at 440 Hz. I use scientific pitch notation, where middle C is C4: Ableton, Logic, Pro Tools and Cubase usually call that C “C3”, FL Studio “C5”. If your DAW shows a different octave, trust the frequency. The [kick and 808 tuning tool](/tools/kick-808-tuning) calculates every note for any key and for other references, such as 432 or 442 Hz.

## The method, step by step

<ol class="steps-list">
<li><div><h3>Find the key of the track</h3><p>If you do not know it, the <a href="/loudness-meter">LUFS meter</a> estimates it from the beat file, and the <a href="/tools/camelot-wheel">Camelot wheel</a> shows you the neighbouring keys. Above all you need the root: in F minor it is F.</p></div></li>
<li><div><h3>See where the root, third and fifth fall</h3><p>In the tuning tool, choose the key: the table shows the notes of the low register with their role. In F minor the root F1 sits at 43.7 Hz, the fifth C2 at 65.4 Hz, the third A♭1 at 51.9 Hz.</p></div></li>
<li><div><h3>Measure the pitch of the kick</h3><p>Solo the kick with a high-resolution spectrum analyzer, or a tuner, and look at the body of the sound, not the initial click. The fundamental is the lowest strong peak, often between 40 and 80 Hz. With the tool’s Hz-to-note converter you find out which note it is and how many cents flat or sharp.</p></div></li>
<li><div><h3>Tune the kick to the root or the fifth</h3><p>With the sampler’s pitch control, move the kick in semitones and cents until the body lands on the root or the fifth of the track. The root gives more weight, the fifth more lift. If the kick drops in pitch as it plays, tune the part that lasts longest, not the attack.</p></div></li>
<li><div><h3>Check the root note of the 808</h3><p>Many 808 samples are labelled with the wrong note, or not labelled at all. Measure them like the kick and set the root note in the sampler, so that playing a C really gives you a C. If you skip this step, the whole bass line will be out of key by the same amount.</p></div></li>
<li><div><h3>Choose the register of the 808 line</h3><p>Notes below roughly 40 Hz disappear on phones, laptops and many headphones, and they eat headroom in the master without being heard. If the line goes too low, consider transposing it up an octave, or letting saturation carry the lowest notes, since it adds harmonics that are audible even on small speakers.</p></div></li>
<li><div><h3>Make the kick and 808 coexist</h3><p>When they play together at close but different pitches, the two waves beat and partly cancel: the hit sounds weaker. You have three options: tune the kick to the same note as the 808 where they coincide; duck the 808 under the kick for 30–80 ms with sidechain; remove the kick where the 808 plays, as is often done in trap.</p></div></li>
<li><div><h3>Check glides and saturation</h3><p>808 glides must start and land on notes of the scale: a glide that lands halfway sounds out of tune. After saturation, check the pitch again, because distortion can make you perceive a different octave from the one you are playing.</p></div></li>
<li><div><h3>Check in mono and on different systems</h3><p>The low end must be mono: with the <a href="/tools/stereo-phase-analyzer">stereo phase analyzer</a> you check the correlation in the sub band. Then listen on a phone, on headphones and, if you can, on a big system. On the phone the rhythm and an idea of the note must remain; on the big system the note must be full.</p></div></li>
</ol>

<p class="warn">Tuning tools use pure sine waves at the lowest frequencies: always start with the volume low, especially on headphones, and raise it slowly.</p>

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I tune the kick in house too?

It depends on the kick. A short, dry kick, typical of classic house, has little pitch: tuning it changes little. A long kick with a lot of body underneath, as in modern techno or in tech house with a “rolling” bass, should be tuned, because its tail is part of the bass line.

### Root or fifth?

The root is the safest choice: it reinforces the key. The fifth works well when the bass line leans heavily on the root and the kick would risk blurring into it. Either way, avoid notes outside the scale: they are the ones that create the muddy low end.

### What if the track changes key?

If the 808 follows the progression, the kick stays tuned to the main key. If the change is long and the kick clashes with the new harmony, you can use two versions of the kick, one per section. It happens more often than you would think in carefully made productions.
