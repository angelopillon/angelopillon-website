A resonance suppressor listens to the signal, finds the narrow peaks that make a sound harsh and pulls them down only when they appear. It is the job I used to do by hand with a dynamic EQ, band by band.

## The comparison

| | Quance | soothe3 |
|---|---|---|
| Release | late October 2026 | 19 May 2026 |
| Price | $79.99 ($49.99 until 30/11) | $259 |
| Lowest latency | zero samples (Live Zero) | low-latency mode |
| Channels | mono and stereo | up to 9.1.6 |
| Protection | Tone Protect and Transient Protect | Soft and Hard modes, tilt controls |
| Trial | 7 days | 20 days |

**Quance.** Press Learn on the part that bothers you and the plug-in places up to eight focus points on the most obvious resonances. Tone Protect tracks fundamentals, harmonics and formants; Transient Protect eases off on the attack of a hit or a consonant. There are five quality grades, from a zero-latency live mode to the heaviest studio mode.

**soothe3.** It merges soothe2’s sharpness and selectivity into a single Detail control, adds a Soft mode with an adaptive threshold, and tilt controls that make the plug-in more or less aggressive on lows and highs within one instance.

## Which one I would pick

For vocals in a mix, Quance at this price makes sense, especially if you track through it and need zero latency. For mastering and multichannel work, soothe3 remains the standard: fine control over lows and highs makes the difference when you work on the whole mix.

<blockquote class="pull">If you can hear the suppressor working, it is working too hard.</blockquote>

Before buying either, try a [well set de-esser](/de-essing-vocals) and some [subtractive EQ](/subtractive-eq): harshness often comes from two or three specific frequencies.
