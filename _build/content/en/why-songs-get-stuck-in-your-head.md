It happens to almost everyone. Researcher Kelly Jakubowski and her team asked 3,000 people which songs most often got stuck in their heads, then compared those melodies with equally popular songs nobody named. The study was published in Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity and the Arts.

## The three traits of an earworm

**A fast tempo.** Quicker songs come back more easily. Jakubowski links this to our urge to move along: many people hear them while running or brushing their teeth.

**A common melodic shape.** The line rises and falls in a way we have heard a thousand times, as in nursery rhymes. The riff of Maroon 5’s “Moves Like Jagger” follows exactly this pattern.

**One detail that stands out.** Inside that familiar shape sits an unusual leap or repetition that makes it recognisable.

Among the most named songs: Lady Gaga’s “Bad Romance”, Journey’s “Don’t Stop Believin’” and, ironically, Kylie Minogue’s “Can’t Get You Out Of My Head”.

## When it happens

Earworms arrive mostly when the mind is idle: in the shower, walking, doing chores. People who listen to and sing a lot of music tend to get more of them. The study also confirmed a long-held suspicion: songs with lots of radio play and recent chart success are more likely to stick.

<blockquote class="pull">An earworm is familiar enough to get in, and strange enough not to leave.</blockquote>
