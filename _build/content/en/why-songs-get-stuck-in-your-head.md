You wake up, make coffee, and a chorus you have not heard for days starts playing in your head. You hum it in the shower, it follows you in the car, it is still there when you go to bed. It happens to almost everyone: a Finnish survey some years ago found that the large majority of people get an earworm at least once a week.

For years it seemed just a curious phenomenon. Then psychology started studying it seriously. Today we know quite well why some songs stick in memory and others do not.

## The earworm study

Researcher Kelly Jakubowski, now at Durham University, and her team asked 3,000 people which songs most often got stuck in their heads. Then they did something clever: they compared those melodies with songs that were just as famous and successful but that nobody had named. That let them isolate the musical features of earworms, independently of popularity. The study was published in Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity and the Arts.

## The three features of an earworm

**A fast tempo.** Faster songs come back more easily. According to Jakubowski it has to do with moving in time: many people hear them while running, walking or brushing their teeth, and the body’s rhythm calls them back.

**A common melodic shape.** The line rises and falls in a way we have heard a thousand times, like the nursery rhymes we learn as children. The riff of Maroon 5’s “Moves Like Jagger” follows exactly this pattern.

**A detail that stands out.** Inside that familiar shape there is an unusual leap or repetition that makes it recognisable. It is the contrast between familiar and unexpected that triggers memory.

Among the songs named most often in the study: Lady Gaga’s “Bad Romance”, Journey’s “Don’t Stop Believin’” and, rather ironically, Kylie Minogue’s “Can’t Get You Out Of My Head”.

## When it happens

Earworms arrive mostly when the mind is not busy: in the shower, walking, doing chores, waiting for the bus. These are automatic activities that leave memory free to “spin” on its own.

Other factors that make them more likely:

- **Recent exposure.** A song heard on the radio this morning is more likely to come back tonight.
- **Repetition.** The study confirmed that songs played often on the radio and recently in the charts stick more easily.
- **Your relationship with music.** People who listen to and sing a lot of music tend to have more earworms.
- **Stress and tiredness.** Many people report more earworms when tired or under pressure.
- **Memories.** A song linked to a place, a person or a moment can come back when something reminds you of them.

## Why the chorus

An earworm is rarely a whole song. It is usually a fragment of a few seconds, almost always the chorus or a riff, repeating in a loop. One possible explanation is that the mind tends to return to things left unfinished: the fragment never closes, so it starts again.

That is also why effective choruses are short, repeated and easy to sing. They are built to get in.

## How to get rid of one

There is no foolproof method, but research has found some tricks that help:

**Chew gum.** A University of Reading study found that chewing reduces involuntary musical thoughts, probably because it occupies the same mechanisms we use to “sing inside”.

**Do something that occupies your mind.** A puzzle, an anagram, a medium-difficulty sudoku. Too easy is not enough; too hard lets the mind wander.

**Listen to the song to the end.** If the earworm is a fragment that never closes, closing it can help.

**Listen to another song.** Some people use a “cure” song, one they know does not get stuck.

<blockquote class="pull">An earworm is familiar enough to get in, and strange enough not to get out.</blockquote>

## What it means for music makers

No formula guarantees a hit, but a few things help a melody to be remembered:

- **A clear melody.** Anyone should be able to hum it after one listen.
- **An unexpected element.** A leap, a pause, a word that breaks the pattern.
- **Space in the mix.** The main melody must sit in front, with no instruments stealing its place.
- **A chorus that opens up.** Compared with the verse, the chorus should feel bigger: wider, fuller, brighter.

In a mix, space is built. A vocal that sits in front, a chorus that widens compared with the verse, a kick and bass that push without covering the melody. I talk about it in the articles on [mixing pop and R&B vocals](/mixing-vocals-pop-rnb) and on [depth in the mix](/depth-in-the-mix).

## Frequently asked questions

**Are earworms a problem?** Almost never. They are a normal experience and usually short-lived.

**Why the songs I do not even like?** A song does not need to be liked to stick: what matters is how it is built and how often you heard it.

**Do musicians get more of them?** People who spend a lot of time with music tend to have more, yes.
