You are listening to a playlist, a song ends and the next one starts: suddenly it sounds quieter, or louder, or “smaller”. You reach for the volume. A few years ago this happened all the time; today far less, thanks to a technology almost nobody knows about: loudness normalisation.

In this article I explain how it works, why the differences do not disappear completely and what it means for music makers.

## A bit of history: the loudness war

Between the nineties and the early 2000s, many labels started asking for ever louder masters. The reason was simple: at the same volume setting, a louder song seems better, fuller, more energetic. On radio, in shops, in CD changers, the louder song won.

To raise loudness, masters were compressed and limited more and more. The result: squashed songs with no dynamics, tiring to listen to for long. It became known as the loudness war.

## What LUFS are

LUFS measure perceived loudness, not peaks. A peak meter tells you how high the signal goes at its loudest point; LUFS tell you how loud the song seems to the ear on average, taking into account how hearing perceives different frequencies.

Platforms use them to turn down tracks that are too loud and, in some cases, turn up quiet ones. That way you move from one song to the next without touching the volume.

## How it works on Spotify

Spotify measures the track when it is uploaded but does not change it: it adjusts the volume while you listen. For albums it uses a single value for all tracks, so quieter songs stay quieter as the artist intended. The web player and many third-party devices, such as TVs and speakers, do not apply normalisation.

| Setting | Level |
|---|---|
| Loud | -11 LUFS |
| Normal | -14 LUFS |
| Quiet | -19 LUFS |

Tracks louder than the chosen level are turned down. Quieter ones are turned up only as far as their peaks allow; on the Loud setting, Spotify also uses a limiter.

## The other platforms

**Apple Music** normalises to -16 LUFS with Sound Check, on by default on recent devices. The same song can sound a little quieter than on Spotify.

**YouTube** turns down tracks louder than about -14 LUFS, but does not turn quieter ones up.

**Tidal** uses a level similar to Spotify and works per album.

## So why do I still hear differences?

**The master.** Two songs at the same average level can sound different: a heavily compressed one seems “fuller” but less alive, a dynamic one more open with stronger hits.

**The genre.** A ballad and a techno track have different frequency content, and the ear perceives them differently even at the same level.

**The platform.** Apple Music normalises to -16 LUFS, so the same song can sound a little quieter than on Spotify.

**Your settings.** If you switched normalisation off, or use the web player, tracks play at their original level.

**Devices.** Some speakers and TVs do not apply normalisation.

<blockquote class="pull">Normalisation ended the loudness war. The winners were those with a well-made master.</blockquote>

## What it means for music makers

**You do not need to crush the master to make it seem louder.** If it is too loud, the platform turns it down, and you risk losing dynamics without gaining level.

**Nor do you need to master exactly to -14 LUFS.** The right level depends on the genre: a club electronic track and an acoustic ballad have different needs. What matters is that the song sounds good, with impact and breath, and holds up next to references in its genre.

**True peak matters.** When the platform converts the file to a compressed format, peaks can rise slightly. I work with a true peak limiter and stop between -0.3 and -0.2 dBTP: safe, and no loudness wasted.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I turn normalisation on?** For mixed playlists, yes: you avoid volume jumps. For an album, album mode keeps the differences the artist intended.

**Why do some old songs sound quieter?** They often have more dynamic, less compressed masters. At the same average level they can seem less “full”, but they breathe more.

**Does normalisation reduce quality?** No. It only changes the volume, as you would with the knob.
