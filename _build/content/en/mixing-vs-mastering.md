<div class="tldr"><span class="box__title">In short</span>
<ul>
<li><strong>Mixing</strong> works on the individual tracks: levels, stereo position, EQ, dynamics, effects and automation, until you have a stereo file.</li>
<li><strong>Mastering</strong> works on the finished stereo file: tonal balance, density, loudness, true peak and consistency between tracks, then it prepares the files for every destination.</li>
<li><strong>Stem mastering</strong> sits in between: a few groups of tracks, more control than stereo mastering, far less work than a full mix.</li>
</ul></div>

Many producers write to me saying “I need mastering” when what they really need is a mix, and some ask for a mix when their track is already balanced and only needs finishing. It is not a question of names: these are two jobs with different goals, tools and timelines. Understanding the difference saves you money and revisions.

## Mixing: decisions about individual elements

A mix starts from the separate tracks: vocal, kick, bass, synths, guitars, effects. Every element has its own channel and its own space. The decisions are many and very specific:

- how loud the vocal should be against the beat, verse by verse;
- where each instrument sits between left and right, and how near or far it feels;
- which frequencies to take out of one element to make room for another;
- how much to compress the kick, the bass, the vocal, and with what character;
- which reverbs and delays to use, and how to automate them over time.

The result is a stereo file in which everything has a place. A good mix already sounds like a record, just a little less dense and less loud.

## Mastering: decisions about the whole

Mastering takes that stereo file. You can no longer raise the vocal without raising everything around it, so the moves are global and more subtle:

- the tonal balance of the whole track, so it sounds right on headphones, in the car, on a phone and on big systems;
- compression and saturation for cohesion and density;
- stereo width and low-frequency control, which must stay solid and mono compatible;
- loudness and true peak, with a limiter that catches the peaks without crushing the groove;
- consistency across the tracks of an EP or album: perceived level, colour, gaps and fades.

Then comes delivery. A master is not just a file that sounds good: it is a WAV at the right resolution, checked after streaming encoding, possibly alongside a version for the club or for live shows.

<blockquote class="pull">Mixing answers the question “how do these sounds sit together?”. Mastering answers “how does this track sit next to all the others?”.</blockquote>

## Why it is better not to do both in the same session

Whoever mixed a track has heard it hundreds of times. They know every detail and, inevitably, stop hearing some things: a vocal slightly low in the chorus, low mids building up, a hi-hat that gets tiring after three minutes. Mastering works best with fresh ears, a different listening setup and a different point of view.

That is why, even when I mix and master the same track, I separate the two in time: I finish the mix, let it rest, and master it as if it came from someone else.

## What mastering cannot fix

Mastering improves a good mix and rescues a decent one. It does not transform a mix with structural problems. A few typical examples:

- **A vocal that is too quiet or too loud.** If I lift it with EQ, I also lift everything that shares its frequencies.
- **Too much reverb.** It cannot be removed from the stereo file without affecting everything else.
- **Kick and bass cancelling each other.** A phase problem between two elements has to be solved at the source.
- **A resonance on a single instrument.** I can reduce it, but I will also hit the other elements in that range.

In these cases it is better to fix the mix, or to move to stem mastering.

## Stem mastering, the middle ground

With stem mastering you send me groups of tracks, usually four to eight (I accept up to 40): drums, bass, vocals, music, effects. I work as in mastering, but I can act on one group without touching the others. It is the right solution when the mix almost fully convinces you and only a couple of elements need fixing: the vocal needs to come forward by a dB, the bass is too wide, the cymbals are harsh.

## What do you need?

- **You like the mix and just want it finished:** mastering.
- **The mix works but one or two elements are out of place:** stem mastering.
- **The balance does not convince you, or you only have the tracks:** mix and master.
- **The vocal needs tuning, timing or cleaning:** mix and master, because tuning is already part of the mix.
