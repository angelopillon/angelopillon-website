Usually the story goes one way: first the hardware, then years later someone models it as a plug-in. With Manley ecliptic it is the other way round. Manley’s first original EQ is born as software, and only if it catches on will it become hardware. For mastering engineers it is interesting news for two reasons: the brand, which for thirty years has meant tubes and transformers, and the approach, because mid-side EQ is one of the most powerful tools there is, and one of the easiest to get wrong.

## The context: who is behind it

In July 2026 Brainworx founder Dirk Ulrich signed the agreements to take Brainworx and Plugin Alliance back after the Native Instruments insolvency. Both brands joined RCKFRC, the family-owned holding that also controls Manley Labs and Apogee. I covered the background in the article on [iZotope, Boris FX and Native Instruments](/izotope-boris-fx-native-instruments).

Together these brands cover the whole chain: converters (Apogee), high-end analogue outboard (Manley), digital modelling (Brainworx and its TMT technology) and plug-in distribution (Plugin Alliance). Manley ecliptic is the first product to use that combination.

## What we know about the plug-in

**A mid-side EQ with one set of controls.** Not two separate equalisers, one for the middle and one for the sides, but a single approach that shapes both. The idea is to make mid-side more musical and less “surgical”.

**Analogue models.** Transformers, filters and equalisers are modelled with Brainworx’s TMT technology, which simulates the small component differences between one channel and the other, as in a real unit.

**Plug-in first, hardware later.** Ulrich explained that the plug-in is a way to gather feedback: if demand is strong enough, a hardware version goes on Manley’s roadmap.

The 6 November 2026 announcement letter gives no price, formats or release date.

## Mid-side, explained simply

A stereo file has two channels: left and right. Mid-side rewrites it differently: **mid** is what the two channels share, the centre (usually vocal, kick, bass, snare); **side** is the difference between them, everything at the edges (reverbs, wide guitars, pads, stereo effects).

Working on centre and sides separately allows moves that are impossible in normal stereo:

- brightening the sides without making the vocal harsher;
- removing low end from the sides for a tighter bass that works on vinyl and in clubs;
- adding body to the centre without inflating the reverbs;
- widening or narrowing the image in a controlled way.

## Why it is easy to get wrong

Mid-side is powerful precisely because it changes the balance between centre and sides, and therefore perceived width. Typical mistakes:

**Sides too bright.** The mix instantly sounds wider and more “expensive”, but in mono it loses the vocal or gets thin.

**Low end on the sides.** A little stereo bass adds size, too much makes the bass unstable and causes trouble in clubs, where the system is often mono below 100-120 Hz.

**A hollow centre.** If the side grows too much relative to the mid, vocal and kick step back.

After every move on the sides I always check [mono compatibility](/phase-and-mono-compatibility). If the track holds up in mono, the move is sound.

## Why one set of controls is a good idea

With two separate EQs it is easy to lose sight of the whole: you lift the top on the sides, compensate in the centre, tweak the sides again, and end up with an incoherent master. An EQ that treats centre and sides together, with one set of controls, forces you to think in terms of balance: how much should the centre weigh against the sides in this band. That is how a good mastering engineer works, and it is why this approach intrigues me.

## How I would use it in mastering

**On electronic music.** Pull a little side below 120 Hz to stabilise kick and bass, then a gentle shelf on the sides above 10 kHz to give air to pads and hi-hats without touching the vocal.

**On pop.** A touch on the mid around 2-4 kHz to bring the vocal forward, and if needed a small side cut in the same band to clean up wide guitars that compete with it.

**On a mix that is already wide.** Instead of widening further, it often pays to reinforce the centre: the mix feels more solid and, paradoxically, deeper.
