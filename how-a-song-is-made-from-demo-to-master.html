How a song is made: from demo to master, step by step

Read inItalianoEnglishDeutsch

When you hear a track on the radio, you are hearing the result of six different jobs. Here is what happens in each one.

By Angelo Pillon 11 April 2025 5 min read

In short
  • There are six stages: writing, production, recording, mixing, mastering, release.
  • Mixing balances the instruments against each other; mastering prepares the finished song for the world.
  • Each stage depends on the one before: a problem left behind gets paid for later.
  • From demo to release usually takes from a few weeks to a few months.

You hear a song on the radio and it lasts three minutes. Behind those three minutes there are often weeks or months of work, and several people passing the song from hand to hand. Each has a specific job, and each can improve the work of whoever came before, but not replace it.

I work on the last two technical stages, mixing and mastering. Every week I see songs arrive in every state, and the difference between a track that takes off and one that struggles is often decided long before it reaches me. Here is the whole journey, from the first idea to the file that ends up on Spotify.

1. Writing

Melody, chords, lyrics. Everything starts here. An idea can come from a guitar riff, a phrase hummed into a phone, a drum loop.

The acid test: a good song holds up played on a single guitar or a piano. If it does not work in that form, no sound will save it. Before moving to production, ask yourself whether the melody is memorable, whether the chorus lands, whether the lyrics say something.

2. Production

The outfit gets chosen: which instruments, which rhythm, which mood. The producer builds the arrangement, picks the sounds and decides how the song grows from start to finish.

Today production and writing often overlap: in electronic music, rap and pop, many songs are born directly in the DAW, starting from a sound or a beat. This is where the demo comes from: a version with the full structure, but not yet polished.

3. Recording

Vocals and instruments are recorded. What matters is capturing the performance well: a suitable microphone, a room that does not boom, the right levels. A good take is worth more than any correction afterwards.

After recording comes editing: choosing the best parts of each take, fixing timing, correcting pitch where needed. Long, invisible work, but essential.

4. Mixing

The mix engineer puts every element in its place: level, tone, space, depth. This is when the individual tracks become a single song.

In practice it means deciding how loud the vocal is against the drums, how kick and bass live together, where each instrument sits in the stereo field, how much reverb each part gets, how the chorus opens up compared with the verse. More in the article on mixing vs mastering.

5. Mastering

The final quality check. Mastering refines the tone, brings the loudness to the right level for the platforms and makes sure the song sounds good on headphones, in the car and in a club. For an album it makes all the songs consistent, so they sound like a family.

It is also the moment for technical details: true peak, formats, ISRC codes, streaming and vinyl versions.

6. Release

The final file goes to a digital distributor, which delivers it to Spotify, Apple Music and the other services. You need artwork, correct metadata, credits and a release date planned in advance. More in the guide to releasing your first song on Spotify.

The summary

Stage Who does it Key question
Writing songwriter does it work with guitar and voice?
Production producer what should it sound like?
Recording engineer, musicians is the performance captured well?
Mixing mix engineer is everything in its place?
Mastering mastering engineer does it sound good everywhere?
Release artist, distributor does it reach the right people?

How long it takes

It varies hugely with the project, but for an independent single the typical times are:

  • Writing and production: from a few days to several months.
  • Recording and editing: from a day to a few weeks.
  • Mixing: usually a few days, with one or two rounds of revisions.
  • Mastering: from 48 hours to a few days.
  • Distribution: better to upload the song at least three or four weeks before release.

The mistakes I see most often

Skipping the guitar-and-voice test. Money goes into producing a song that does not stand on its own.

Recording badly because “it will get fixed in the mix”. Mixing improves sound; it does not rebuild a performance.

Mixing and mastering alone with no second opinion. After weeks on the same song, the ear gets used to its flaws.

Rushing the release. A planned release date gives time for promotion and playlists.

Each stage can improve the one before. None can replace it.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to do everything in a professional studio? No. Writing, production and much recording happen at home today. For mixing and mastering, an outside ear and reliable monitoring are worth it.

Should the same person mix and master? They can, but a second pair of ears at mastering helps. I do both, and when a mix from someone else arrives I listen to it before starting.

What should I send my mix engineer? Well-exported tracks, all from the same start point, with clear names. Everything is in the guide to exporting tracks for mixing and mastering.
