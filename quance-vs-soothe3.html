CodWaves Quance vs soothe3: two resonance suppressors compared

  • CodWaves Quance came out in late October 2026: $79.99, or $49.99 until 30 November.
  • oeksound soothe3 came out on 19 May 2026 at $259, with a $55 upgrade from soothe2.
  • Quance bets on price, zero latency and automatic protection; soothe3 on fine control, refinement and multichannel up to 9.1.6.

In recent years resonance suppressors have become one of the most used tools in modern mixing. Harsh vocals, whistling guitars, synths that “sting” at high volume, a kick that booms on one note: they are all narrow resonance problems, frequencies that stick out too much at one precise point. A few years ago you fixed them by hand, with a dynamic EQ and a lot of patience. Today there are plug-ins that do it automatically.

oeksound’s soothe created the category and became a standard. In late October 2026 CodWaves released Quance, which costs a third as much and promises not to eat your transients. In this article I compare them and explain when they make sense and when they do not.

What a resonance suppressor does

A normal EQ cuts a frequency all the time, even when it is not needed. A dynamic EQ cuts it only when it crosses a threshold. A resonance suppressor goes further: it analyses the spectrum in real time, finds narrow peaks that stick out from neighbouring frequencies and pulls them down only when they appear, across dozens of bands at once.

Used well, the result is a softer, less tiring sound that keeps its brightness. Used badly, the sound becomes flat, dull and lifeless: resonances are also part of an instrument’s character.

Quance in detail

Learn. Press Learn on the part that bothers you and the plug-in places up to eight focus points on the most obvious resonances. It is fast and intuitive, especially if you do not want to spend hours hunting frequencies.

Tone Protect. It tracks fundamentals, harmonics and formants, the frequencies that give a voice or instrument its identity, and shields them from reduction. It answers the classic “sound that loses character” problem.

Transient Protect. It eases off the reduction on the attack of a hit or a consonant, so drums stay punchy and vocals keep their articulation.

Five quality grades. From a zero-latency live mode, useful if you track or perform through the plug-in, up to the heaviest and most precise studio mode.

soothe3 in detail

Detail. It merges soothe2’s sharpness and selectivity into one control that sets how narrow and how targeted the processing is.

Soft mode. It adds an adaptive threshold: the plug-in adjusts itself to the material, with a gentler behaviour.

Tilt controls. They make the plug-in more or less aggressive on lows and highs within one instance. Essential on a whole mix, where low and high end have different needs.

Multichannel. Up to 9.1.6, so it also works in Dolby Atmos.

Where I would use them, instrument by instrument

Vocals. The main use case. A vocal recorded with a bright mic in a small room often has resonances between 2 and 5 kHz. A light suppressor, after corrective EQ and before compression, makes the vocal pleasant even when you push it up.

Distorted guitars and synths. Distortion creates harmonics that pile up in specific areas. Here you want a light but broad touch.

Drums and overheads. Cymbals can turn harsh. With Quance’s Transient Protect or a soft soothe3 setting you control them without losing the attack.

Mix bus and mastering. This needs finesse: small moves, separate control of lows and highs, great care for character. This is soothe3’s territory.

If you can hear the suppressor working, it is working too hard.

Mistakes to avoid

Using it as the first move. Fix the recording first, then subtractive EQ. The suppressor is a finishing tool.

Overdoing it. Always compare at matched level: a quieter sound always seems softer. If you toggle it without compensating, you fool yourself.

Putting it on everything. Ten instances on ten tracks flatten the mix. Two or three targeted moves are better.

Which one I would pick

For vocals in a mix, Quance at this price makes sense, especially if you track through it and need zero latency. For mastering and multichannel work, soothe3 remains the standard: fine control over lows and highs makes the difference when you work on the whole mix.

Before buying either, try a well set de-esser and some subtractive EQ: harshness often comes from two or three specific frequencies.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use them while recording? Quance yes, thanks to its zero-latency mode. soothe3 has a low-latency mode.

Do they replace a de-esser? Partly. For sibilance a dedicated de-esser is often more precise and predictable.

Which has the longer trial? soothe3, 20 days against Quance’s 7.
